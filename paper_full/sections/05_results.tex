\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All differences are in nDCG@10 unless stated otherwise, and every $p$-value comes from the
paired bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}.

\subsection{Baseline Hybrid Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-baseline}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{The standard hybrid pipeline on SciFact (300 test claims), stage by stage. All variants
share one corpus, one passage split and one index. R@100 counts relevant abstracts within the
top 100 passages; ms per claim is approximate.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccr@{}}
\toprule
Variant & nDCG@10 & R@10 & R@100 & MRR@10 & ms \\
\midrule
BM25            & 0.656 & 0.784 & 0.882 & 0.623 & 80 \\
Dense (pretrained MiniLM) & 0.679 & 0.815 & 0.916 & 0.641 & 22 \\
RRF fusion ($w = 1$) & 0.710 & 0.833 & 0.947 & 0.675 & 119 \\
\; + cross-encoder reranking & 0.715 & 0.833 & 0.947 & 0.685 & 329 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the baseline. BM25 reaches 0.656, close to the 0.665 reported by
BEIR for SciFact \parencite{thakur2021beir}, which confirms that the passage split and the
evaluation behave as intended. The pretrained dense retriever's 0.679 is not significantly
better ($+0.023$, $p = 0.25$). The two retrievers are tied on average but succeed on different
claims: for 17 claims only BM25 places a relevant abstract in its top ten, and for 25 only the
dense retriever does. RRF exploits this disagreement and reaches 0.710, significantly above BM25
($+0.053$, $p < 10^{-4}$) and the dense retriever ($+0.031$, $p = 0.019$). Reranking the top 50
fused passages adds $+0.006$, which is not significant ($p = 0.65$), at almost three times the
latency (329 against 119~ms). This standard pipeline, at 0.715, is the baseline for the
comparison.

\subsection{Performance of the Proposed Retriever on SciFact}
\label{sec:res-improvement}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{From the standard pipeline to the proposed retriever on SciFact (nDCG@10, 300 test
claims). $^{*}$: significantly different from the row it is compared with in the text (paired
bootstrap, $p < 0.05$).}
\label{tab:improvement}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}Lccr@{}}
\toprule
Configuration & nDCG@10 & R@10 & ms \\
\midrule
Standard pipeline: pretrained dense + BM25, RRF ($w = 1$), reranker & 0.715 & 0.833 & 329 \\
Stronger off-the-shelf encoder (BGE-small), dense only & 0.723 & -- & -- \\
Fine-tuned MiniLM, dense only & 0.742$^{*}$ & -- & -- \\
Fine-tuned MiniLM + BM25, RRF ($w = 1$) & 0.731 & -- & -- \\
\textbf{Proposed: fine-tuned MiniLM + BM25, RRF ($w = 0.3$), no reranker} & \textbf{0.759}$^{*}$ & \textbf{0.905} & \textbf{114} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Fine-tuning raises the dense retriever's nDCG@10 on the development claims from 0.691 to 0.770,
levelling off after epoch 9, and on the test claims from 0.679 to 0.742 ($+0.063$,
$p < 10^{-4}$). The fine-tuned encoder is the best single retriever in the study and is level
with the larger off-the-shelf BGE-small encoder ($p = 0.23$), at no extra cost per query.

A stronger dense retriever changes the effect of fusion. With equal weights, fusing the
fine-tuned encoder with BM25 falls slightly below the encoder alone (0.731 against 0.742,
$p = 0.44$): once one retriever dominates, an equal vote for the weaker one adds noise. With the
weight selected on the development claims, $w = 0.3$, the proposed retriever reaches an nDCG@10
of \textbf{0.759} and a Recall@10 of 0.905 (Table~\ref{tab:improvement}). It is significantly
better than the standard pipeline ($+0.043$, $p = 0.003$) and than equal-weight fusion with the
same encoder ($+0.027$, $p = 0.0009$), and level with the fine-tuned encoder alone ($+0.017$,
$p = 0.057$). It requires about 114~ms per claim against 329~ms for the standard pipeline,
because it has no reranker. Adding the reranker to the proposed retriever lowers its score to
0.745. These results address RQ1: on SciFact, the proposed retriever is more accurate than the
standard pipeline at about a third of its cost.

\subsection{Analysis of the Fusion Weight}
\label{sec:res-weight}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{Selection of the BM25 weight on the 120 development claims (fine-tuned encoder). $w = 0$
is dense retrieval alone; $w = 1$ is standard fusion; the break-even weight of Proposition~1 is
0.381.}
\label{tab:weights}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccccccc@{}}
\toprule
$w$ & 0 & 0.1 & 0.2 & \textbf{0.3} & 0.5 & 0.7 & 1 & 1.5 & 2 \\
\midrule
nDCG@10 & 0.770 & 0.778 & 0.781 & \textbf{0.788} & 0.786 & 0.785 & 0.772 & 0.762 & 0.755 \\
R@10 & 0.887 & 0.887 & 0.887 & \textbf{0.912} & 0.896 & 0.904 & 0.883 & 0.867 & 0.858 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fusion_weight_curve.png}
\caption{Development nDCG@10 against the BM25 weight $w$, with the fine-tuned encoder on each
dataset. The dashed line is the break-even weight $(k + 1)/(k + K) = 61/160 = 0.381$ of
Proposition~1; in the shaded region BM25 can only re-order the dense candidates. The circled
point is the weight selected on the development set and used on the test set. The y-axes differ.}
\label{fig:weights}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:weights} and Figure~\ref{fig:weights} show the weight search on the development
claims of both datasets. On SciFact the score rises from the encoder alone ($w = 0$) to a maximum
at $w = 0.3$, stays nearly flat up to $w = 0.7$, and falls once BM25 weighs as much as the dense
retriever or more. Proposition~1 explains the maximum: at $w = 0.3$, below the break-even weight
of 0.381, BM25 cannot bring any passage into the fused list and only re-orders the encoder's
candidates. The test claims confirm this, since no abstract in the proposed retriever's fused
list lies outside the dense retriever's list. The cost of the restriction is small: only two test
claims have a relevant abstract in BM25's top ten that the dense retriever does not retrieve.

The development scores do not drop sharply at the break-even weight, since weights of 0.5 and
0.7 score within 0.003 of the best; the proposition does not predict a sharp threshold. It
explains the best weight and provides a rule for selecting it when development claims are few:
when the dense retriever is the stronger one, the weight of the weaker retriever should remain
below $(k + 1)/(k + K)$. With the pretrained encoder, where the two retrievers are equally
strong, the weight makes no difference (0.711 at $w = 0.3$ against 0.710 at $w = 1$, $p = 0.88$),
which is consistent with this reading and addresses RQ2.

\subsection{Generalisation to NFCorpus}
\label{sec:res-nfcorpus}
% Numbers: results/nfcorpus/, results/nfcorpus_finetuned__fusion_weighted/,
% results/nfcorpus_finetuned__fusion_weighted__rerank/,
% results/nfcorpus_scifact_encoder__fusion_weighted/, results/fusion_weights_all-MiniLM-L6-v2-nfcorpus.json,
% results/finetune_all-MiniLM-L6-v2-nfcorpus/history.json, results/nfcorpus_comparisons.json.

The same method was applied, unchanged, to NFCorpus (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}): the encoder was
fine-tuned on NFCorpus's training queries, the BM25 weight was selected on its 120 development
queries, and the result was evaluated once on its 323 test queries (Table~\ref{tab:nfcorpus}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{The same method on NFCorpus (323 test queries). P@10 is reported next to nDCG@10
because each query has many relevant abstracts; ms per query is approximate.}
\label{tab:nfcorpus}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}Lcccr@{}}
\toprule
Configuration & nDCG@10 & P@10 & R@100 & ms \\
\midrule
BM25 & 0.312 & 0.227 & 0.201 & 12 \\
Dense, pretrained MiniLM & 0.330 & 0.251 & 0.252 & 13 \\
Standard fusion ($w = 1$) & 0.340 & 0.253 & 0.254 & 31 \\
Standard pipeline (fusion + reranker) & \textbf{0.358} & 0.260 & 0.254 & 168 \\
\midrule
Dense, fine-tuned on NFCorpus & 0.336 & 0.258 & \textbf{0.293} & 13 \\
\textbf{Proposed: fine-tuned + BM25 at $w = 0.2$, no reranker} & 0.350 & \textbf{0.267} & \textbf{0.293} & \textbf{29} \\
\quad + reranker & \textbf{0.358} & 0.266 & \textbf{0.293} & 171 \\
SciFact encoder, not retrained ($w = 0.3$) & 0.343 & 0.253 & 0.253 & 29 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

On NFCorpus, unlike SciFact, the reranker improves the standard pipeline significantly
($+0.019$ over standard fusion, $p = 0.0045$). The proposed retriever reaches the same level
without it: 0.350 against 0.358, a difference that is not significant ($p = 0.35$), in about 29
instead of 168 milliseconds per query, with the highest precision in the top ten of all
configurations (0.267). Adding the reranker to the proposed retriever gives no significant gain
($+0.008$, $p = 0.23$). The selected weight is 0.2, again below the break-even weight, and the
development curve follows Proposition~1 more sharply than on SciFact: it peaks at $w = 0.2$
(0.385) and falls steadily above the break-even point (0.376 at $w = 0.5$, 0.357 at $w = 1$,
0.335 at $w = 2$; Figure~\ref{fig:weights}). As the proposition predicts, the proposed
retriever's Recall@100 equals that of the fine-tuned encoder (0.293).

Fine-tuning contributes less on NFCorpus. It raises the encoder's Recall@100 from 0.252 to 0.293
but its nDCG@10 only from 0.330 to 0.336 ($p = 0.49$), because the queries are short titles
written for the general public and the training queries carry many uniformly graded judgements,
which are weak labels. The SciFact-trained encoder, applied without retraining, does not improve
on standard fusion either ($+0.003$, $p = 0.56$). With respect to RQ3, the weighted-fusion
component transfers to the second dataset, whereas the benefit of fine-tuning depends on the
quality of the domain's training labels.

\subsection{Effect of Claim--Evidence Term Overlap}
\label{sec:res-overlap}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{overlap_split.png}
\caption{nDCG@10 on claims whose terms overlap little (left group in each panel) or much (right
group) with their evidence abstract, split at the median overlap of 0.57, for the pretrained
(left) and the fine-tuned (right) dense encoder.}
\label{fig:overlap}
\end{figure}

The property of a claim that best separates the retrievers is its word overlap with the evidence
abstract, the share of the claim's terms that occur in the abstract. With the claims split at the
median overlap of 0.57 (Figure~\ref{fig:overlap}), the pretrained dense retriever outperforms BM25
by 0.073 on the 156 low-overlap claims ($p = 0.022$), whereas on the 144 high-overlap claims BM25
is at least as accurate (0.889 against 0.857, $p = 0.13$); fusion is never worse than the better
of the two. Fine-tuning raises dense retrieval by 0.106 on the low-overlap claims
($p < 10^{-4}$) but only by 0.016 on the high-overlap ones ($p = 0.21$): the encoder learns the
paraphrases that word matching misses. Because the overlap is computed with the relevant
abstract, this analysis is descriptive and is not available to a retriever at query time.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Computational Cost}
\label{sec:res-sensitivity}

The other design choices do not change these conclusions. Reranking more or fewer fused passages
changes cost but not accuracy: nDCG@10 stays between 0.716 and 0.722 for depths from 10 to 100,
while latency grows from about 120~ms without reranking to about 470~ms at depth 100. Fusion is
insensitive to its constant ($k \in \{10, 30, 60, 100\}$ gives 0.709 to 0.714), and score fusion
is level with RRF (0.717 against 0.710, $p = 0.27$). With whole abstracts instead of passages,
the dense retriever loses 0.031 ($p = 0.009$), because 71.6\% of the abstracts exceed the
encoder's 256-token limit. Keeping the sentences that follow a short window adds 118 passages and
changes no result significantly (0.7586 against 0.7587 for the proposed retriever, $p = 0.63$).
In terms of cost, the BM25 index and the passage embeddings are built once in under three minutes
(32.3~MB of vectors), fine-tuning takes about ten minutes on the laptop's GPU, and reranking 50
candidates adds 160 to 260~ms per claim, which makes the reranker the largest saving available
in the pipeline.
