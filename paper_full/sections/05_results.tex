\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All differences are in nDCG@10 unless stated otherwise, and every $p$-value comes from the
paired bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}. The results answer the research questions in
order: the standard pipeline that serves as the baseline, the improvement (RQ1), why its
weight works (RQ2), the second dataset (RQ4), which retriever helps which claim (RQ3), and
the cost and quality of what is built on top. RQ5 is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:failures}.

\subsection{The baseline: the standard hybrid pipeline}
\label{sec:res-baseline}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{The standard hybrid pipeline on SciFact (300 test claims), stage by stage. All
variants share one corpus, one passage split and one index. R@100 counts relevant abstracts
within the top 100 passages; ms per claim is approximate.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccr@{}}
\toprule
Variant & nDCG@10 & R@10 & R@100 & MRR@10 & ms \\
\midrule
BM25            & 0.656 & 0.784 & 0.882 & 0.623 & 80 \\
Dense (pretrained MiniLM) & 0.679 & 0.815 & 0.916 & 0.641 & 22 \\
RRF fusion ($w = 1$) & 0.710 & 0.833 & 0.947 & 0.675 & 119 \\
\; + cross-encoder reranking & 0.715 & 0.833 & 0.947 & 0.685 & 329 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows the baseline. BM25 reaches 0.656, close to the 0.665 that
BEIR reports for SciFact \parencite{thakur2021beir}, which indicates that the passage split and
the evaluation behave as intended. The pretrained dense retriever's 0.679 is not significantly
better ($+0.023$, $p = 0.25$; better on 73 claims, worse on 60).

The two retrievers are tied on average but not on the same claims: for 223 claims both place a
relevant abstract in their top ten, for 17 only BM25 does, for 25 only the dense retriever
does, and for 35 neither does. Fusion exploits this disagreement. RRF reaches 0.710,
significantly above BM25 ($+0.053$, $p < 10^{-4}$) and the dense retriever ($+0.031$,
$p = 0.019$), and raises recall within the top 100 passages to 0.947. Reranking the top 50
fused passages adds $+0.006$, which is not significant ($p = 0.65$; 52 claims better, 46
worse), at almost three times the latency (329 against 119~ms). This standard pipeline, at
0.715, is the baseline the improvement is measured against.

\label{sec:res-improvement}

\end{figure}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{From the standard pipeline to the improved retriever on SciFact (nDCG@10, 300 test
claims). $^{*}$: significantly different from the row it is compared with, as stated in the
text (paired bootstrap, $p < 0.05$).}
\label{tab:improvement}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccr@{}}
\toprule
Configuration & nDCG@10 & R@10 & ms \\
\midrule
Standard pipeline: pretrained dense + BM25, RRF ($w = 1$), reranker & 0.715 & 0.833 & 329 \\
Stronger off-the-shelf encoder (BGE-small), dense only & 0.723 & -- & -- \\
Fine-tuned MiniLM, dense only & 0.742$^{*}$ & -- & -- \\
Fine-tuned MiniLM + BM25, RRF ($w = 1$) & 0.731 & -- & -- \\
\textbf{Improved: fine-tuned MiniLM + BM25, RRF ($w = 0.3$), no reranker} & \textbf{0.759}$^{*}$ & \textbf{0.905} & \textbf{114} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Fine-tuning raises the dense retriever's nDCG@10 on the development claims from 0.691 to
0.770, levelling off after epoch 9 (Figure~\ref{fig:finetune}), and on the test claims from
0.679 to 0.742 ($+0.063$, $p < 10^{-4}$). This makes it the best single retriever in the
study, level with the larger off-the-shelf BGE-small encoder ($p = 0.23$), at no extra cost
per query.

A stronger dense retriever changes what fusion can do. With equal weights, fusing the
fine-tuned encoder with BM25 falls slightly below the encoder alone (0.731 against 0.742,
$p = 0.44$): once one retriever dominates, an equal vote for the weaker one adds noise.
Fusion helps again when that vote is reduced. With the weight chosen on the development
claims, $w = 0.3$, the improved retriever reaches \textbf{nDCG@10 0.759} and Recall@10 0.905
(Table~\ref{tab:improvement}). It is better than the standard pipeline ($+0.043$, $p = 0.003$)
and than equal-weight fusion with the same encoder ($+0.027$, $p = 0.0009$), and level with the
fine-tuned encoder alone ($+0.017$, $p = 0.057$). It needs about 114~ms per claim against 329
for the standard pipeline, because it has no reranker. Adding the reranker to the improved
retriever lowers its score (0.745), so the improvement is not one that a reranker can build on.
The answer to RQ1 is therefore positive on SciFact: in-domain fine-tuning with weighted fusion
retrieves evidence significantly better than the standard pipeline, at about a third of its
cost.

\subsection{Why the weight works (RQ2)}
\label{sec:res-weight}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{Choosing the BM25 weight on the 120 development claims (fine-tuned encoder). $w = 0$
is dense retrieval alone; $w = 1$ is standard fusion; the break-even weight of
Proposition~1 is 0.381.}
\label{tab:weights}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccccccc@{}}
\toprule
$w$ & 0 & 0.1 & 0.2 & \textbf{0.3} & 0.5 & 0.7 & 1 & 1.5 & 2 \\
\midrule
nDCG@10 & 0.770 & 0.778 & 0.781 & \textbf{0.788} & 0.786 & 0.785 & 0.772 & 0.762 & 0.755 \\
R@10 & 0.887 & 0.887 & 0.887 & \textbf{0.912} & 0.896 & 0.904 & 0.883 & 0.867 & 0.858 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:weights} shows the search on the development claims. The score rises from the
encoder alone ($w = 0$) to a maximum at $w = 0.3$, stays nearly flat up to $w = 0.7$, and
falls once BM25 weighs as much as the dense retriever or more. Proposition~1 explains the
maximum: at $w = 0.3$, below the break-even weight of 0.381, BM25 cannot bring any passage into
the fused list and only re-orders the encoder's candidates. The test claims confirm this: no
abstract in the improved retriever's fused list lies outside the dense retriever's list. The
price is small: only two test claims have a relevant abstract in BM25's top ten that the dense
retriever does not retrieve at all, among them ``Ivermectin is used to treat
onchocerciasis'', which BM25 ranks first.

The development scores do not drop sharply at the break-even weight; weights of 0.5 and 0.7
score within 0.003 of the best. The proposition therefore does not predict a cliff. What it
provides is an explanation of the best weight, and a rule for choosing it when development
claims are few: when the dense retriever is the stronger one, keep the weight of the weaker
retriever below $(k + 1)/(k + K)$, so that fusion can only re-order the stronger retriever's
candidates. With the pretrained encoder, where the two retrievers are equally strong, the
weight makes no difference (0.711 at $w = 0.3$ against 0.710 at $w = 1$, $p = 0.88$),
which is consistent with this reading: re-ordering helps only when the candidates are
already good.

\subsection{Which retriever helps which claim? (RQ3)}
\label{sec:res-overlap}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{overlap_split.png}
\caption{nDCG@10 on claims whose terms overlap little (left group in each panel) or much
(right group) with their evidence abstract, split at the median overlap of 0.57, for the
pretrained (left) and the fine-tuned (right) dense encoder.}
\label{fig:overlap}
\end{figure}

To see where the retrievers differ, the 300 test claims are split by properties computed from
their text. The property that separates the retrievers most is the word overlap between a
claim and its evidence abstract: the share of the claim's terms, after BM25's tokenisation,
that occur in the abstract. With the split at the median of 0.57 (Figure~\ref{fig:overlap}),
the pretrained dense retriever beats BM25 by 0.073 on the 156 low-overlap claims, whose
evidence is phrased differently ($p = 0.022$). On the 144 high-overlap claims BM25 is at least
as good (0.889 against 0.857, $p = 0.13$), and fusion gains 0.052 over the dense retriever
($p < 10^{-4}$). Fusion is never worse than the better of the two retrievers. Paraphrased claims
are also much harder: no method exceeds 0.53 on them in the standard pipeline, against 0.86 to
0.92 on the others.

The split also shows what fine-tuning teaches. It raises dense retrieval by 0.106 on the
low-overlap claims ($p < 10^{-4}$) but only by 0.016 on the high-overlap ones ($p = 0.21$): the
encoder learns the paraphrases that word matching misses. The overlap is computed with the
relevant abstract, so it describes the claims and is not available to a retriever at query
time.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the other design choices}
\label{sec:res-sensitivity}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{nDCG@10 when one component of the standard pipeline is changed. $^{*}$: significantly
different from the standard pipeline for the same variant ($p < 0.05$).}
\label{tab:configs}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
Configuration & BM25 & Dense & Fusion & + Rerank \\
\midrule
Standard (pretrained MiniLM) & 0.656 & 0.679 & 0.710 & 0.715 \\
Whole abstracts instead of passages & 0.658 & 0.648$^{*}$ & 0.703 & 0.687$^{*}$ \\
Score fusion instead of RRF & -- & -- & 0.717 & -- \\
Fusion by abstract instead of passage & -- & -- & 0.706 & 0.704 \\
BGE-small encoder & 0.656 & 0.723$^{*}$ & 0.721 & 0.712 \\
\quad with the BGE reranker & -- & -- & -- & 0.714 \\
Passages keep the sentences after a short window & 0.656 & 0.678 & 0.710 & 0.715 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

None of the other choices changes the picture (Table~\ref{tab:configs}). Reranking more or
fewer fused passages buys cost, not accuracy: nDCG@10 stays between 0.716 and 0.722 for depths
from 10 to 100, while latency grows from about 120~ms without reranking to about 470~ms at
depth 100. Fusion is insensitive to its constant ($k \in \{10, 30, 60, 100\}$ gives 0.709 to
0.714), and score fusion is tied with RRF (0.717 against 0.710, $p = 0.27$); RRF is kept
because it needs no score normalisation. With whole abstracts instead of passages, BM25 is
unchanged but the dense retriever loses 0.031 ($p = 0.009$), because 71.6\% of the abstracts
exceed the encoder's 256-token limit and are truncated, against 1.2\% of the passages. Finally,
keeping the sentences that follow a short window (Section~\ref{sec:chunking}) adds 118 passages
and changes no result significantly: BM25 and fusion are identical on all 300 claims, and the
improved retriever scores 0.7586 against 0.7587 ($p = 0.63$).

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:res-cost}

Building the BM25 index over the 21,015 passages took 18 to 41~s and encoding them 53 to
130~s with MiniLM; the embedding index occupies 32.3~MB. Per claim, BM25 took about 60 to
120~ms because it scores every passage, dense retrieval 16 to 32~ms, and reranking 50
candidates added 160 to 260~ms (about 1.2~s with the BGE reranker). Fine-tuning is a one-off
cost of about ten minutes on the laptop's GPU. Removing the reranker is therefore the largest
saving available in the pipeline, and the improvement makes it possible without losing
accuracy.

Retrieval is half of question answering, so the answers built on it were scored too: 50 test
claims, two readers, four criteria from 0 to 2, with a rubric written before any answer was
generated. The scores were assigned with AI assistance, by the assistant that had run both
readers, so the scoring is not blind (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Table~\ref{tab:answers}
shows a clear trade-off. The generative reader is more often right (correctness 0.84 against
0.48), but a third of its answers state something the cited passage does not say, such as
calling albendazole ``an alkylating agent used for multiple myeloma''. The extractive reader
never says anything unsupported, because it copies text, but mostly returns fragments that do
not answer the claim.

\subsection{Could retrieval decide when to abstain?}
\label{sec:res-abstention}

Neither reader abstains: for the 9 of the 50 claims whose relevant abstract was not among the
five passages, both returned an answer instead of ``not enough evidence''. A retrieval signal
could trigger abstention, but not cheaply. Over all 300 test claims, the top cross-encoder
score separates claims with a relevant abstract among the five passages from those without
one with an AUC of 0.81 (top dense similarity: 0.73). Yet the threshold chosen on the
development claims (Equation~\eqref{eq:youden}) catches 68 of the 74 misses only by also
refusing 109 of the 226 answerable claims. A reader that abstains needs more than a score
threshold, such as the trained evaluators reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lit-abstain}.
