\section{Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:failures}

Aggregate scores show how often retrieval fails but not why. This chapter examines the claims
on which the standard pipeline fails most clearly, assigns each failure a cause, and then asks
which of these failures the improved retriever repairs (RQ5).

\subsection{Selecting the claims}
\label{sec:fail-selection}

The claims were chosen by a fixed rule before any was examined. Every test claim receives a
priority
\begin{equation}
\pi(q) = 2\,\bigl(1 - \mathrm{R@10}_{\mathrm{full}}(q)\bigr)
+ 1.5\,\mathbb{1}\bigl[\mathrm{R@10}_{\mathrm{fusion}}(q) > \mathrm{R@10}_{\mathrm{full}}(q)\bigr]
+ \bigl|\mathrm{R@10}_{\mathrm{BM25}}(q) - \mathrm{R@10}_{\mathrm{dense}}(q)\bigr|,
\label{eq:priority}
\end{equation}
where ``full'' is the standard pipeline with the reranker: the first term rewards claims the
pipeline misses, the second claims for which the reranker lost an abstract that fusion had
found, and the third claims on which the two retrievers disagree. The 40 claims with the
highest priority were analysed. The selection is deliberately the hardest claims, not a random
sample, so the shares below describe hard failures rather than all errors. For 39 of the 40,
the standard pipeline has no relevant abstract in its top ten; the fortieth has two relevant
abstracts, of which the reranker pushed one out.

\subsection{Categories and their frequency}
\label{sec:fail-categories}

Each claim was assigned one of six categories fixed in advance: terminology mismatch (the
evidence uses different words), ambiguous claim, missing evidence (the abstract marked as
evidence does not state the claim), conflicting evidence, granularity (right abstract, wrong
passage) and reranker error (fusion found the evidence and the reranker demoted it). For each
claim the evidence abstract, its rank under every variant and the abstracts ranked first were
read. The labels were assigned with AI assistance following these definitions and have not
yet been validated by a second annotator (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{Causes of failure on the 40 hardest test claims of the standard pipeline, and how
many of them the improved retriever repairs (a relevant abstract in its top ten).}
\label{tab:failures}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Category & Claims & Repaired by the improvement \\
\midrule
Missing evidence in the marked abstract & 17 (42\%) & 9 \\
Terminology mismatch & 11 (28\%) & 7 \\
Reranker error & 8 (20\%) & 5 \\
Ambiguous claim & 4 (10\%) & 1 \\
Conflicting evidence, granularity & 0 & -- \\
\midrule
Total & 40 & 22 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:failures} shows the result. The largest group is not a retrieval error at all:
for 17 claims the abstract marked as evidence does not state what the claim says. Three pairs of
contradictory claims share a single marked abstract (for example ``PPAR-RXRs are inhibited by
PPAR ligands'' and ``PPAR-RXRs can be activated by PPAR ligands''), and that abstract never
mentions PPAR. With judgements made per abstract, such claims cap every retriever's score.

Terminology mismatch accounts for 11 claims, which share few words with their evidence (median
overlap 0.25). The evidence for ``Aspirin inhibits the production of PGE2'' speaks only of COX
inhibitors, so every retriever of the standard pipeline misses it; in another claim ``SFM'' is
expanded as a rare syndrome while its abstract is about severe falciparum malaria. In the 8
reranker errors, fusion had the evidence in its top ten and the cross-encoder pushed it out: for
``Ivermectin is used to treat onchocerciasis'', BM25 ranks the evidence first and fusion third,
but the reranker drops it to seventeenth. No failure was attributed to conflicting evidence or
to granularity.

\subsection{Which failures the improvement repairs}
\label{sec:fail-repaired}

The last column of Table~\ref{tab:failures} counts, for each category, the claims for which the
improved retriever places a relevant abstract in its top ten. It repairs 22 of the 40 failures.
As expected, it repairs most of the reranker errors (5 of 8), since it has no reranker, and most
of the terminology mismatches (7 of 11), the paraphrases that fine-tuning teaches the encoder
(Section~\ref{sec:res-overlap}). It also repairs 9 of the 17 claims labelled as missing evidence,
which by definition no retriever should be able to repair. Two explanations are possible, and
the data cannot separate them: the fine-tuned encoder may have learned regularities of how
SciFact's annotators linked claims to abstracts, or some of these labels may be wrong. Either
way, this result marks the missing-evidence category as the one most in need of the independent
second annotation listed among the limitations.

\subsection{Examples}
\label{sec:fail-examples}

Three claims illustrate the categories and how the stages behave on them.

\begin{description}
\item[Terminology mismatch.] \emph{``Aspirin inhibits the production of PGE2.''} The evidence
abstract discusses COX inhibitors without the word aspirin. BM25 has no shared term to match,
and the pretrained encoder does not connect the drug to its class; all variants of the standard
pipeline miss the abstract, and so does the improved retriever: 689 training claims do not teach
the encoder every drug-to-class relation, which is where a biomedical encoder trained at scale,
such as MedCPT \parencite{jin2023medcpt}, could help.
\item[Reranker error.] \emph{``Ivermectin is used to treat onchocerciasis.''} BM25 ranks the
evidence first and fusion third, but the cross-encoder, trained on web search, demotes it to
seventeenth. With the improved retriever's weight of 0.3, BM25 can only re-order the dense
candidates (Proposition~1), and the fine-tuned encoder does not retrieve this abstract, so the
improvement does not repair this claim either: it is one of the two test claims whose evidence
only BM25 finds.
\item[Missing evidence.] \emph{``PPAR-RXRs are inhibited by PPAR ligands.''} and its opposite
share one marked abstract that never mentions PPAR; no ranking of abstracts can make both
claims correct.
\end{description}
