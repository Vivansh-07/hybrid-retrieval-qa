\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of the Results}
\label{sec:disc-results}

The gain of the proposed retriever over the standard pipeline ($+0.043$ nDCG@10 on SciFact) has
two sources, which Table~\ref{tab:improvement} separates. Fine-tuning contributes most of it: the
encoder alone rises from 0.679 to 0.742, which already exceeds the reranked pipeline. Weighted
fusion adds a further $+0.017$, whereas equal-weight fusion with the same encoder loses 0.011
(0.731). The difference between the two fusion settings is significant ($p = 0.0009$) and is
explained by Proposition~1: at $w = 0.3$ BM25 can only re-order the encoder's candidates, while
at $w = 1$ its weaker candidates displace good dense ones.

This mechanism makes two observations in the literature precise. \textcite{wang2026blend} report
that one weak retrieval path can degrade a fused result; Proposition~1 identifies the weight
below which this cannot happen in weighted RRF. \textcite{bruch2024fusion} found that a tuned
fusion weight outperforms the default; here the tuned weights on both datasets (0.3 and 0.2) lie
below the derived bound of 0.381, so the bound can replace the search when development data are
scarce. The complementarity of lexical and dense retrieval reported by \textcite{chen2022hybrid}
holds for the pretrained encoder, where fusion adds $+0.031$ over dense retrieval, but after
fine-tuning BM25 is useful mainly as a re-ordering signal.

The reranking result differs from benchmarks in other domains. \textcite{akarsu2026bm25} found
hybrid retrieval followed by reranking to be the strongest configuration for financial question
answering; on SciFact, the web-trained cross-encoder adds only $+0.006$ to the standard pipeline
($p = 0.65$) and lowers the score of the proposed retriever (0.745 against 0.759). For scientific
claims, a small encoder adapted to the domain is therefore more effective than a reranker trained
on web search. On NFCorpus the reranker does improve the standard pipeline ($+0.019$,
$p = 0.0045$), yet the proposed retriever reaches the same level without it, even though
fine-tuning contributes little there ($+0.006$) because of the dataset's short lay queries and
uniformly graded training judgements.

\subsection{Implications for Literature Assistants}
\label{sec:disc-tools}

Three implications follow for retrieval-augmented assistants such as SciSpace and Consensus.
First, adapting a small first-stage encoder to the domain with a few hundred labelled claims
improved evidence retrieval more than a web-trained reranker, at about a third of the cost per
query. Second, when one retriever is clearly stronger, the weight of the other should be kept
below $(k + 1)/(k + K)$, so that fusion re-orders the stronger retriever's candidates without
importing weaker ones. Third, keyword search remains useful for claims worded like their evidence
and for exact identifiers such as gene and drug names, where BM25 is strongest. None of these
conclusions requires access to the internals of a commercial tool, since each concerns a design
choice that every retrieval-augmented assistant makes.
