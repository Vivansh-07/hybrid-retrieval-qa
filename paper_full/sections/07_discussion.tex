\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\label{sec:disc-rq}

\paragraph{RQ1: can the improvement beat the standard pipeline at lower cost?} On SciFact, yes.
The fine-tuned encoder fused with BM25 at $w = 0.3$ reaches an nDCG@10 of 0.759 against 0.715
for the standard pipeline with a reranker ($p = 0.003$), and a Recall@10 of 0.905 against
0.833, at about 114 instead of 329 milliseconds per claim. The gain comes from two sources that
add up: fine-tuning teaches the encoder the paraphrases that separate scientific claims from
their evidence, and weighted fusion lets BM25 sharpen the order without bringing in its weaker
candidates. The reranker, the most expensive stage, becomes unnecessary; added to the improved
retriever, it even lowers the score.

\paragraph{RQ2: why does the weight work?} Proposition~1 shows that below
$(k + 1)/(k + K) = 61/160$, a BM25-only passage can never outrank a dense candidate, so fusion
becomes a re-ordering of the dense list. The chosen weight of 0.3 lies below this bound, and on
the test claims no fused abstract lies outside the dense list. The proposition does not predict
a sharp drop above the bound (weights up to 0.7 score almost as well on the development claims);
it explains what the best weight does and gives a rule for setting it without a search when one
retriever is clearly stronger.

\paragraph{RQ3: which retriever helps which claim?} Word overlap decides. Dense retrieval wins
on paraphrased claims (+0.073, $p = 0.022$), BM25 holds on claims worded like their evidence, and
fusion is never worse than the better of the two. Fine-tuning closes most of the gap on
paraphrased claims ($+0.106$ there), which is why, after fine-tuning, BM25 is useful only as a
re-ordering signal.

\paragraph{RQ4: does the improvement hold on a second dataset?} Partly. On NFCorpus the
recipe again chooses a weight below the break-even point (0.2), weighted fusion again improves
on the encoder alone ($p < 10^{-4}$), and the reranker again becomes unnecessary: the improved
retriever is statistically level with the reranked pipeline (0.350 against 0.358, $p = 0.35$)
at about a sixth of its time per query. It does not beat that pipeline, as it does on SciFact,
because fine-tuning helped little there (+0.006, not significant): NFCorpus's lay queries and
its many weakly judged training abstracts give poorer training signal than SciFact's
expert-written claims. The fusion component of the improvement is general; the fine-tuning
component is only as good as the training labels of the domain.

\paragraph{RQ5: where does retrieval still fail?} Of the 40 hardest failures of the standard
pipeline, 42\% are claims whose marked evidence does not state them, 28\% are vocabulary
mismatches, 20\% reranker errors and 10\% ambiguous claims. The improved retriever repairs 22
of the 40, most of the reranker errors and vocabulary mismatches among them. What remains
are failures of the benchmark's judgements, of vocabulary that 689 claims cannot teach, and of
claims whose only lexical evidence BM25 finds.

\subsection{What the findings mean for literature assistants}
\label{sec:disc-tools}

The findings concern the retrieval step that literature assistants such as SciSpace and
Consensus depend on, and five of them carry over to such systems directly.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Adapt the encoder before adding a reranker.} A small encoder fine-tuned on a few
hundred in-domain examples did more than a reranker trained on web search, at a third of the
cost. For a tool that serves many queries, this is the cheaper way to better evidence.
\item \textbf{Weight the weaker retriever below the break-even point.} Equal-weight fusion
helps only when the two retrievers are comparably strong; once one is stronger, its partner
should only re-order, which Proposition~1 shows how to guarantee.
\item \textbf{Keep keyword search.} Even after fine-tuning, BM25 finds evidence that the dense
retriever misses on claims worded like their evidence, and identifiers such as gene and drug
names are where it is strongest.
\item \textbf{Measure faithfulness, not only citations.} A generative reader that cites the
right paper can still state something the paper does not say, as a third of the generative
answers did here; citation accuracy alone does not show this.
\item \textbf{Let the reader abstain, but do not rely on one score for it.} The reranker's
confidence predicts missing evidence (AUC 0.81), but a threshold on it refuses about half of
the answerable questions; abstention needs a trained component.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent None of this requires access to the tools' internals: each finding concerns a design
choice that any retrieval-augmented assistant makes, measured here on known evidence.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:disc-validity}

\paragraph{Internal validity.} The fusion weight and the fine-tuning epoch were chosen on
development claims disjoint from the test claims, and the test claims were used once per
configuration, so the reported gains are not the result of tuning on the test set. Many
configurations were compared without a correction for multiple comparisons, so $p$-values near
0.05 should be read with care; the main comparison ($p = 0.003$) is well below that.

\paragraph{External validity.} SciFact's claims are short, expert-written and biomedical, with
about one relevant abstract each. The second dataset (Section~\ref{sec:res-nfcorpus}) differs
in query style and in the number of relevant abstracts; other domains, longer documents and much
larger corpora remain untested.

\paragraph{Construct validity.} Relevance is judged per abstract, so a passage-level pipeline is
evaluated at the abstract level; the failure analysis shows that some judgements themselves
are questionable. The answer scores and failure labels were assigned with AI assistance, by one
annotator, following fixed definitions.
